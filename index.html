Good to know

  • Back each key up with its Back up button. If this browser's data is cleared or you change device, anything sent to the key is lost unless you have the backup file and its passphrase.
  • A passkey (button on the key's card) lets a fingerprint, face or PIN bring the passphrase back, through Reveal with passkey. The passphrase still works. A passkey stays on the device that made it and is not in the backup.
  • Keys do not sync between browsers or devices. Use the backup to move one.

Help: how it works, when to use it, and which apps keep the image

How it works

  1. Encrypt. Your browser encrypts the secret (text, a file or a pasted picture) with your passphrase: AES-256-GCM, with a key from Argon2id, which is deliberately slow and memory-hungry so that guessing is expensive. Your choices (reveal animation, reading time, expiry) are sealed inside the same encrypted data. Instead of a shared passphrase it can be locked to one or more public keys: the same random key is wrapped once for each recipient, so only the holder of a matching private key can open it, and the passphrase never has to travel. An optional recipient pass code is a second way in.
  2. Sign (optional). A separate Ed25519 signing key signs the complete encrypted packet. Only a signing fingerprint you checked with its owner through a trusted channel identifies a sender. Encryption alone does not. Valid signatures from unknown keys and unsigned messages are labelled accordingly; invalid signatures are refused. Signing adds 108 bytes and exposes the public signing key to anyone who can extract the packet.
  3. Hide. The encrypted bytes, with error correction added, are drawn into an image: a code-art mosaic, a QR code (a phone camera opens this page with the secret ready for the passphrase), a fake QR code (it scans as a harmless decoy while the real data hides in the error-correction budget) or an ordinary-looking photo. One photo can hold up to 4 messages, each with its own passphrase and pass code, and a secret that is too big is split across several images.
  4. Send. You send the image like any picture: pasted, attached or scanned.
  5. Reveal. The recipient pastes, drops, picks or scans the image on the Reveal tab and types the passphrase (or pass code), or, for an image sent to their public key, the passphrase of their own key (or a passkey, if they added one). The original comes back with an animation, and clears itself from the screen after the reading time. Nothing is uploaded: there is no server.

How to use

Sending

  1. On Hide a secret, type or paste your text (or choose a file, paste a picture, or read the text from a camera photo) and enter a passphrase, or press Generate.
  2. Pick an Image style: code art (works almost everywhere), QR code (opens this site when scanned), fake QR code (scans as a decoy) or hidden in a photo (looks like an ordinary picture; the JPEG-safe method survives normal recompression). You can choose your own cover photo.
  3. To authenticate yourself, make an Ed25519 signing key once in My keys, back it up and share its public half. Under Sign as a sender, select that key and enter its passphrase for this creation. The signing passphrase stays with you. Recipients must check your signing fingerprint through a trusted channel.
  4. Optionally open More options: a reveal animation (one of 84), a reading time (which you can lock so the recipient cannot change it), an expiry from 1 minute to 30 days, and a recipient pass code. With the photo style you can also add up to 3 more messages to the same photo, each with its own passphrase. Or open Send to a public key instead to lock it to someone's public key, so no passphrase has to be shared (they make a key once under My keys and contacts and send you the public half).
  5. Press Create image, then Copy message (the image, a link to this site and, for code art and QR, a direct link to the secret) and paste it into an email or chat, or download the image. Give the passphrase to your recipient by a different route.

Receiving

For sender identity, save the sender's Ed25519 public key in My keys, compare its displayed words with them through a trusted channel and mark it checked. Require a verified sender rejects unsigned messages and other signing keys before revealing anything. A name or an unknown key's valid signature alone does not establish who sent it.

  1. Open the link, use the Reveal secret button of the Outlook add-in, or use this page's Reveal a secret tab: paste (Ctrl+V), drop or choose the image, or scan a QR code with the camera. If the secret was split, add every part.
  2. Type the passphrase (or the pass code) and press Reveal. If the secret was sent to your public key, the box changes to Your key's passphrase: type the passphrase you chose when you made your key (not a shared secret). Your key has to be saved on this device, or restored first from its backup in the My keys tab. Watch the animation (or press Skip), read the secret, copy it or save the file. It self-destructs after the reading time: when the time is up the revealed text goes up in a puff of smoke on screen and a vortex sucks it away, and the extracted text and any picture are swallowed with it. (Clear now clears at once.)

Public keys

  1. Open the My keys tab and Make a new key: choose a name and a passphrase for it (and, if your device supports it, tick Also keep the passphrase safe with a passkey). The private half stays on your device.
  2. Back the key up, then give people your public key, its QR code or the "send me a secret" link. Compare the nine-word fingerprint with them by a call or in person, so you know nobody swapped the key.
  3. To send to someone, open Send to a public key instead on the Hide tab, paste their public key (or open their link) and create the image as usual. No passphrase has to be shared.
  4. To open one sent to you, add the image on the Reveal tab and type your key's passphrase, or press Reveal with passkey. On a new device, restore the key from its backup first.

Use it inside Outlook (add-in)

An email program cannot run the decryption itself, so VeilVia messaging has an Outlook add-in: a Reveal secret button that opens a pane beside the email, picks up its image attachment (or secret link) and asks for the passphrase. Everything runs on your device.

  2. Paste the manifest link above and confirm. If your Outlook does not accept a link, press Download file instead and choose Add from file (browse to the downloaded manifest.xml).

A Microsoft 365 admin can add the same manifest under Integrated apps for a whole team. The pane loads Microsoft's office.js (Outlook needs it); this page loads nothing from third parties. The add-in has been tested with a stand-in for Outlook, not yet inside every real Outlook, and some organisations block custom add-ins.

When to use this

  • Good for: sending a password, API key, recovery phrase or private note over a channel you do not fully trust (email, chat), when you can give the passphrase by a different route (a phone call, in person), or, with public keys, when the recipient has made a key and sent you the public half. Also for hiding text in plain sight inside a photo.
  • Not for: anything that must stay safe against someone who gets the image and can guess the passphrase. They can try guesses offline for as long as they like, so use a long random passphrase (the Generate button makes one).
  • Not for: long-term storage or anything you cannot afford to lose: there is no recovery if the passphrase is forgotten. It is also no replacement for an end-to-end encrypted messenger or a password manager, and an expiry is a courtesy rather than a lock.

Which apps keep the image intact

What matters is whether an app delivers your image as it is or re-compresses and shrinks it like a photo.

How the image travelsCode art, QR, fake QRPhoto, JPEG-safePhoto, lossless
Sent as a file: email attachment, "send as file" or "document" in chat apps, cloud-drive linkYesYesYes
Re-compressed as JPEG at the same sizeYes (tested down to JPEG quality 50)Yes (tested down to quality 50)No
Shrunk, as many chat apps do to photosCode art: yes (tested at 60% size plus JPEG). QR and fake QR: usually, while still big enough to scanNoNo
Screenshot of the whole imageYesUsually, if it is not scaledOnly if exact pixels
Cropped, rotated or only part of itNoNoNo

In practice: attachments and "send as file/document" options (email, Telegram, WhatsApp, Signal) generally deliver the original. Sending as a normal photo in most chat and social apps (WhatsApp, iMessage, Messenger, Instagram, and Telegram or Signal photo mode) re-compresses it and may shrink it, so use Code art or a JPEG-safe photo there. Pasting inline into an email usually works but some mail programs resize large pictures. Apps differ and change their behaviour between versions, and not every app has been tested: send yourself a test image first.
